# Lesson 1 · Introduction

[简体中文](/textbook/L01/) ｜ [繁體中文](/zh-hant/textbook/L01/) ｜ **English** ｜ [日本語](/ja/textbook/L01/) ｜ [한국어](/ko/textbook/L01/)

## 1. What the eight characters are

### 1.1 What a chart is

The eight characters are the year, month, day and hour of your birth written out in the ten heavenly stems and the twelve earthly branches.

The old writers marked time with ten stems (jia, yi, bing, ding, wu, ji, geng, xin, ren, gui) and twelve branches (zi, chou, yin, mao, chen, si, wu, wei, shen, you, xu, hai). A year takes one pair, a month one pair, a day one pair, an hour one pair: four points in time, two characters each, eight characters in all.

These four pairs are called the four pillars:

| Pillar | Stands for | What it governs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Year | The root of the family | Origins and the standing of the household |
| Month | Parents and household | The surroundings one grew up in, and the early run of luck |
| Day | The person himself | Temper, capacity and marriage |
| Hour | Children and later life | Old age, and the lot of one’s children |

One example. A person born on 25 May 2026 at 14:00 has the eight characters bing-wu, gui-si, ji-hai, xin-wei. Pillar by pillar:

- Year pillar bing-wu: 2026 is a bing-wu year. This pillar shows the wider circumstances and the root of the family.
- Month pillar gui-si: 25 May falls after the start of summer; the si month is in command and fire is on the rise. This pillar shows the household and the parents.
- Day pillar ji-hai: the day stem is ji earth, and that ji earth is the day master, standing for the person himself. The day pillar is the centre of the four.
- Hour pillar xin-wei: 14:00 is the wei hour. This pillar shows later life and the tie to children.

The eight characters together make up the chart. It is a base map of a life, fixed at birth, and everything afterwards is argued from it.

### 1.2 Solar, lunar and stem-branch calendars

A chart is set out by the stem-branch calendar. There is no need to worry if you hold only a solar birthday or only a lunar one: the three calendars convert into one another, and given the right rules the chart comes out the same whichever you start from. Beginners go wrong not by using the lunar date but by not knowing that the chart is set from the stem-branch calendar, and by mistaking the boundary rules — taking the first day of the lunar year for the turn of the year, or matching solar months straight onto branches.

**1. The solar calendar.** The Gregorian calendar in ordinary use, one year being one circuit of the earth round the sun, about 365.25 days. Months and dates roughly match the position of the sun on the ecliptic.

- Day to day: identity papers, work schedules, international business.
- Relation to the chart: not used directly, but charting software usually asks for the solar date and converts it.

**2. The lunar calendar.** The traditional Chinese lunisolar calendar. Months follow the moon, about 29.5 days from new moon to new moon, and leap months (seven in nineteen years) keep the years in step with the sun. The Spring Festival, the Dragon Boat Festival and the Mid-Autumn Festival are all counted by it.

> A caution: the lunar calendar is often called the yin calendar, but strictly it is not a pure lunar one. A pure lunar calendar, such as the Islamic, has no leap month and does not keep step with the solar year, so its months drift through the seasons. The Chinese calendar has the twenty-four solar terms and leap months precisely because it also follows the sun.

- Day to day: festivals, the almanac, farming.
- Relation to the chart: a lunar birth time, correctly converted, gives exactly the same chart. Note that the first day of the lunar year is not the start of spring in the stem-branch calendar, and that is where hand conversion goes wrong.

**3. The stem-branch calendar.** The calendar proper to charting. It rests entirely on the position of the sun, that is, on the twenty-four solar terms, and has nothing to do with the first day of a lunar month.

- The year begins at the start of spring, not at the lunar new year and not on 1 January.
- The month is divided by solar terms: from the start of spring to the awakening of insects is the yin month, from the awakening of insects to clear and bright is the mao month, and so on.
- The day begins at the zi hour, 23:00 to 01:00.
- The day is divided into twelve double hours, each the equivalent of two hours today.

| Calendar | Basis | New year begins | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solar (Gregorian) | The sun, one tropical year | 1 January | Papers, international business |
| Lunar | The moon, with leap months to keep step with the sun | First day of the first lunar month | Festivals, the almanac, farming |
| Stem-branch | The twenty-four solar terms | Start of spring | Setting out a chart |

> **Pitfall one.** “I was born on the first day of the lunar year, so I must be a horse.” Not necessarily. The stem-branch year turns at the start of spring, not at the lunar new year. In 2025 the start of spring fell on the sixth day of the first lunar month, at the hai hour (3 February, 22:10:13). Anyone born from the first to the fifth day of that lunar month still belongs to the previous jia-chen dragon year. In 2026 the start of spring fell on the seventeenth day of the twelfth lunar month, at the yin hour (4 February, 04:01:51): the lunar month was still the last of the old year, but spring had begun, and the stem-branch year had entered bing-wu, the horse. The animal of the year is decided at the start of spring.
> **Pitfall two.** “I was born on 4 February, so why does the chart give the previous year?” Because the year turns at the moment of the start of spring, to the second, not on the date. In 2026 that moment is 4 February, 04:01:51. Born before it, the stem-branch year is still yi-si, the snake; born after it, bing-wu, the horse. The same calendar date gives two different years.
> **Pitfall three.** Not knowing that the chart uses the stem-branch calendar, and laying lunar or solar months straight onto the branches. The stem-branch month is cut by solar terms and coincides with neither. The first lunar month is not the yin month; January is not the zi month. The safe course is to enter the solar birth time into charting software and let it convert.

The safe procedure when setting out a chart:

1. Get the solar birth time — year, month, day, hour, to the minute if possible.
2. Check whether daylight saving time was in force; it matters for births between 1986 and 1991.
3. Check the place of birth, for true solar time. China keeps one clock, at 120 degrees east, but local solar time varies with longitude: Urumqi, at 87 degrees east, runs about two hours behind Beijing, so the same clock time gives a different hour pillar. Enter the birthplace and let the software correct it.
4. Let the software convert. Do not work it out by hand.
5. Check the day pillar. It is the test of whether the chart has been set correctly; if the day pillar is wrong, the year or the hour is off.

> **In short.** The chart rests on the stem-branch calendar, and a solar or lunar birth time, correctly converted, gives the same result. The year turns at the start of spring. The months are cut by solar terms, the yin month beginning at the start of spring and the mao month at the awakening of insects. Set the chart to the double hour, and where possible check it against a birth certificate or hospital record; those born between 1986 and 1991 should establish whether daylight saving time applied.

> *Jishan Pian*, opening: “Man receives his frame from heaven and earth, his fate from yin and yang. He lives within the covering and the carrying, and is altogether inside the five phases. To know rank, look first at the month command as the framework; to judge fortune, use the day stem as the master.” Four lines that carry the whole programme of the subject: the month command fixes the pattern, the day stem is oneself. The fifteen lessons that follow turn on those ten words.

## 2. Fate and luck

On fate and luck, nothing written in the last thousand years is more vivid than the *Han Yao Fu* of Lü Mengzheng of the Northern Song. Lü lived as a young man in a broken kiln with not enough for three meals, then took first place in the examinations and served three times as chancellor. The whole of that life is in the piece, which is at once advice to a prince and the liveliest gloss there is on fate and luck.

### 2.1 Fate: what is given

Fate is made before birth and does not change. It is the make and capacity of the vehicle: a good car or a farm tractor, settled at the factory. It sets the ceiling a life can reach, the level and the general frame, and no effort afterwards moves it much.

What counts as given? Whatever was fixed at the moment of birth: the family one was born into, the state of the parents, one’s place among the brothers and sisters, the condition of the body, the weather of that hour. None of it is chosen and none of it reversed. The chart fixes that instant.

> From the *Han Yao Fu*: “Wealth and stipend are already arranged within fate. If a man did not rest on the eight characters of his root, how could he become a minister?” High and low, poor and rich, have their measure in the chart — not as superstition, but as the plain existence of a frame.

### 2.2 Luck: what comes after

Luck is what is met after birth, and it moves. It is the road the vehicle runs on: a clear motorway, little resistance and good speed; a muddy track, jolts and difficulty at every step. It sets how favourable each stretch of a life is, and there are good stretches and hard ones.

What counts as after? Everything that can change once one is born — the institutions of the time, the economy, schooling, technology, the work one takes up, the city one settles in. All of that can be worked at.

> One person is born to a comfortable family and idles his way to nothing; another is born poor, comes of age as the country opens up, and works his way to a fortune. A chart does not say whether a life is good; it says where the start is made and how the stages are best walked. The chart fixes the instant of birth; luck decides what is made of it.
> From the *Han Yao Fu*: “A horse may have a thousand-li road in it, but without a rider it cannot go of itself; a man may have a resolve that reaches the sky, but without luck he cannot get through.” Fate gives the ceiling; luck decides how much of it is cashed.

### 2.3 The four combinations

Fate and luck together give four courses:

**1. Good fate, good luck** — a good car on a motorway. Everything runs with him, and the advantage of birth is enlarged by the times.

**2. Good fate, bad luck** — a good car on a muddy track. Born at the wrong time, with no ground to use his gifts on. The *Han Yao Fu* names them: Confucius pressed at Chen, Li Guang never ennobled to the end — not for want of ability, but for want of luck.

**3. Ordinary fate, good luck** — a tractor on a motorway. Not the best vehicle, and still it gets somewhere, sometimes past a better car with no road. Lü Mengzheng himself: begging at a temple in the morning, sleeping in a kiln at night, and one examination later among the three highest officers of state — “this was the time, this was the luck, this was fate.”

**4. Poor fate, poor luck** — a tractor in the mud. A hard life, though hard work may still bring it to the end of the road; the road is only longer and steeper than other people’s.

> A note by Chujia Kuang. Fate and luck is the first lesson and the central one. In good luck, take the opening and go with it; in bad luck, hold the ground and gather strength. Learning the subject is not for resigning to a fate but for choosing well on whatever road one is on.
> Beginners are in a hurry to change their fate, which puts the cart before the horse. First know it — see plainly what is given and what the times offer; then make it — move at the right hour and go with the grain. Fate cannot be changed; luck can be chosen.
> Four kinds of waiting, which I give every student: wait while learning, wait for the moment to appear, wait for others to make their mistakes, wait for the opening to come. Before good luck arrives, waiting is practice; while bad luck runs, waiting is holding. And a thing I say over and over: fullness invites loss, modesty receives gain; aim at the officer in charge, not at the emperor in full bloom.

### 2.4 Two attributes of a chart

A chart has two sides.

The first is natural — what is given, and the root of health, like the species of a tree and the depth of its roots, which cannot be exchanged. The direct officer in the dimension of health shows as pressure and hidden trouble; the eating god as length of life; the seven killings with nothing to control them as an attack on the body. All of that is the natural side, the level of fate.

The second is social — success and disgrace, standing in the world, which depend on the times, the ground, the people and the intangible turn of luck woven together. The direct officer in the dimension of work is office and authority; the direct wealth in the dimension of kin is wife and money. The ten god has not changed, and the social reading shifts with circumstance: the same direct officer is power in government, credit in business, reputation in scholarship.

The natural side fixes the body of a chart and the social side its use. The body cannot be exchanged; the use can be directed. This is the ground, in the philosophy of the subject, for one chart showing different faces in different dimensions — the doctrine of one body, many faces.

    The social side of a chart depends on four things woven together: the times (the epoch), the ground (resources and position), the people (a team and its loyalty) and the unseen turn (luck and the opening). The historical cases below show what each of the four weighs.
    **All four: Zhu Yuanzhang.** The founding emperor who began lower than any other in Chinese history — orphaned young, a monk, a beggar at the bottom of society. The Yuan was coming apart (the times); he took Nanjing first and held the Yangtze (the ground); he gathered Xu Da, Chang Yuchun and Liu Bowen (the people); at Poyang Lake Chen Youliang was struck by a stray arrow (the turn). With all four, a beggar became Son of Heaven. His chart need not have been of the first rank; the decades of his luck brought all four together and pressed the chart to its ceiling — a chart has a ceiling, and the four can push it to the top.
    **Liu Bang and Liu Xiu.** Liu Bang rose from a village constable in Pei county in the turmoil at the end of Qin, entered the passes first, gathered the three heroes of the early Han and escaped at Hongmen: four in place, and four hundred years of Han. Liu Xiu rose from a decayed branch of the imperial house at the end of Wang Mang, held Hebei as his base, gathered the twenty-eight generals of Yuntai, and won at Kunyang against odds: four in place, and the Han restored. Neither began high; with the four, both prevailed.
    **The ground: Qin.** The plain inside the passes, with Hangu to the east, Wu to the south, Dasan to the west and Xiao to the north, was easy to hold and hard to take. The Zhengguo canal watered ten thousand hectares and filled the granaries. Coalition after coalition came against it and could not get through the pass. Six generations of labour, and the ground did the rest.
    **The people: Liu Bei.** A weaver of mats and seller of sandals. The times were Cao Cao’s and the ground was Sun Quan’s; what he had was people. Guan Yu and Zhang Fei in the peach garden, Zhuge Liang at the third visit, and a name for benevolence that drew men to him. On that one word the weakest of the three held a third of the empire.
    **Times against him: Yang Guang.** The Sui emperor cut the canal between north and south and created the examinations, both of them right and lasting. Then three campaigns against Goguryeo drained the state, and the times were not with him: after years of war the country wanted quiet, not change. The right thing, at the wrong time, ended with the empire in revolt and the emperor dead. That is the cruelty of luck: the thing correct, the ability sufficient, the decade wrong, and everything returns to nothing.
    **The unseen turn: Zhu Di at Jingnan.** The prince of Yan rose against the court with fewer troops and the name of a usurper. At the Bai river a north wind came up as the southern army was breaking, and sand and stones drove them back on the wind; at Jiahe the wind came again and the southern standard snapped. Several times the wind turned certain defeat into escape. That is the unseen turn: what cannot be planned and cannot be foreseen, arriving at the decisive hour.
    **All four lost: Chongzhen.** The last Ming emperor worked harder than most of his line, up before dawn and never out of his clothes, and it availed nothing. The times were against him: the little ice age, famine year on year, risings in the provinces, the Qing beyond the pass. The people were lost: faction in the court, Yuan Chonghuan killed and no one left to use. The ground was lost: rebel armies everywhere and the capital surrounded. The turn was absent: plague, locusts and drought together. He hanged himself at Meishan, not for want of ability or of will, but because the four had gone.
    A reading of a chart looks at how these four stand in it. Zhu Yuanzhang reversed the lowest start with all four in place; Yang Guang held an empire and lost it with the times against him; Chongzhen worked day and night and lost everything with all four gone. Of the four, one missing is a difficulty, two is a danger, all four is the end.
    These historical readings are the author’s own view, offered to make the four terms concrete; the reader may judge them for himself.

### 2.5 The logic of the direct officer

In a chart, what checks me and differs from me in polarity is the direct officer. The old writers counted it the first of the fortunate gods. The *Yuanhai Ziping* opens by calling it a thing of noble qi, and the *Wanqi Fu* calls the officer star the master that honours the person and the source from which stipend flows. Because it governs standing and name, the subject is in substance an argument about the direct officer.

In the social dimension the direct officer is central. Every point on the path by which a person succeeds in the world belongs to it: one’s own capacity is its root, the patron who recognises one is its support, the platform of work is what carries it, power and place are its fruit, and a good name is where it comes to rest.

It does not follow that an officer means wealth and honour. Whether the officer can act depends on whether it has strength in the chart — whether wealth comes to generate it and the seal to shield it. The detail belongs to later lessons.

> A note by Chujia Kuang. The subject is, at bottom, the logic of the direct officer: see whether a person has one, whether it has strength, whether it has support, and the course of his working life can be judged. That is why the lessons that follow return again and again to the officer as the root of honour.

## 3. The Zhouyi as the source

### 3.1 The Zhouyi and the eight trigrams

The *Zhouyi* was put together in the Western Zhou and is honoured as the source of the great way and the first of the classics. It has two parts, the text and the commentaries: the text holds the sixty-four hexagrams with their judgments and line statements, the commentaries are later expositions of them. Its central thought is that everything under heaven is in ceaseless change, and that the change follows rules — which is the philosophical ground of every later art of prediction.

> From the *Xici* commentary: “In the Changes there is the great ultimate, which gives the two forms; the two forms give the four images; the four images give the eight trigrams; the eight trigrams settle fortune; fortune gives rise to the great work.” Eleven characters of logic, from the ultimate to the trigrams and from the trigrams to fortune.

The *Zhouyi* works with eight trigrams. The old observers of nature settled on eight basic conditions: qian is heaven, firm and strong; kun is earth, yielding; kan is water, perilous; li is fire, bright; zhen is thunder, arousing; xun is wind, penetrating; gen is mountain, still; dui is marsh, glad. The trigrams are not eight isolated signs but a way of sorting the ten thousand things into eight kinds and working out how they move, by combining one with another. That habit of reasoning by signs, and of letting the signs carry reality, is what the later chart method inherited.

### 3.2 From the Zhouyi to the chart

The *Zhouyi* at first had only hexagrams and judgments, with no yin and yang, no five phases, no stems and branches. From the early Western Zhou to the Song, the chart method took nearly two thousand years to form. Four moments mark the path.

**First: from hexagram to the five phases (Warring States).** Zou Yan of Qi, about 324 to 240 BC, systematised the five phases and put forward the succession of the five powers, holding that dynasties replace one another in the order wood, fire, earth, metal, water. His contribution was to weld yin and yang and the five phases, until then separate notions, into one logic of generation and check that could explain anything.

After him the trigrams were given phases: qian and dui metal, kun and gen earth, zhen and xun wood, kan water, li fire. Prediction stopped resting on what a hexagram looked like and began to reason about which phase generates and which checks. That is the step from thinking in images to thinking in logic.

**Second: stems and branches brought into the trigrams (Western Han).** Jing Fang, 77 to 37 BC, devised the method of attaching the stems, bringing the ten stems and twelve branches into the system so that every line of every hexagram carried its pair. This was the first deep joining of the calendar signs to the hexagrams.

The stems and branches had been day-counting signs since the Shang. Over a thousand years they had grown into the full cycle of sixty. Once the phases were matched to them — jia and yi wood, bing and ding fire, and so on — a complete coordinate of time against phase existed, and any moment could be turned into a set of phases and argued by generation and check. That is the working principle of charting.

**Third: three pillars (Tang).** Li Xuzhong first joined the stems and branches of the year, month and day to the five phases and used them to judge a life. The hour pillar was still missing, but the whole chain, from birth time to signs to phases to fate, had been walked for the first time. Later generations honour him as the founder.

**Fourth: four pillars (Song).** In the early Northern Song, Xu Ziping added the hour pillar to Li’s three, giving the frame of year, month, day and hour, eight characters. One pillar added, and much changed: the chart moved from the circumstances of a day to the moment of birth, and its accuracy with it. The method has been called Ziping’s art since.

> Zhouyi, reasoning by hexagram; Zou Yan, the five phases and the logic of generation and check; Jing Fang, the stems and branches as a coordinate of time; Li Xuzhong, three pillars; Xu Ziping, four. Each step took prediction from the vague towards the exact.
> Two other branches grew from the *Zhouyi*: six lines, one question to a divination, and plum blossom numerology, which takes its hexagram from whatever is at hand. With the four pillars they make the three branches of the art, one source and three streams.

### 3.3 The two schools of Zhouyi study

The old students of the *Zhouyi* divided in two. The school of image and number reasoned from hexagrams and numerical patterns, and valued image and number. The school of meaning and principle expounded the sense of the judgments, and valued meaning and principle. The chart method uses both: it reads image and number in the generation and check of the phases, and it argues meaning and principle in what a life amounts to.

## 4. Schools and texts

### 4.1 Where the chart came from

Li Xuzhong in the Tang began judging by the three pillars of year, month and day, and is honoured as the founder. Xu Ziping in the Song added the hour and fixed the frame of four pillars and eight characters; the art has borne his name since. Through the Song, Ming and Qing, schools multiplied and texts appeared, and the subject came to maturity.

### 4.2 Seven books worth reading

Seven works have had the widest influence:

**Ditian Sui**, Song, Jing Tu. The scripture of the subject: compact in wording and deep in argument. Ren Tieqiao’s Qing commentary, the *Ditian Sui Chanwei*, is the chief work of the school of strength and decline.

**Yuanhai Ziping**, Song, Xu Dasheng. An early classic, taking the pattern as the centre of judgment; the foundation of the pattern school. The name in the title honours Xu Ziping.

**Sanming Tonghui**, Ming, Wan Mingying. A compilation that gathers what others had said, the most complete system of its time, with a great many formulae and cases.

**Shenfeng Tongkao**, Ming, Zhang Shenfeng. The doctrine of illness and medicine: a chart has its illness, an imbalance of the phases, and needs its medicine, the useful god. Direct in manner, and given to cases.

**Ziping Zhenquan**, early Qing, Shen Xiaozhan. The high point of pattern theory, reasoning and method held together, and the most exact statement of the pattern school.

**Ditian Sui Chanwei**, Qing, Ren Tieqiao. Strength and decline of the day master as the first test, from which useful and taboo gods follow. Simple and widely used.

**Qiongtong Baojian**, late Qing, anonymous. Also known as *Lanjang Wang* and *Zaohua Yuanyao*. It judges by the adjustment of the season: what the month’s weather asks of each stem, with the phases read season by season.

### 4.3 Five schools

Five schools have formed, each with its texts and its leading idea:

**Pattern.** On the *Yuanhai Ziping* and the *Ziping Zhenquan*. Read what pattern a chart makes — direct officer, seven killings and so on — and rank it by success and failure. The most traditional and the most systematic.

**Illness and medicine.** On the *Shenfeng Tongkao*. Where the phases are out of balance is the illness; the useful god that corrects it is the medicine. Heavy illness and light medicine is hard to treat; the right medicine and the illness goes.

**Strength and decline.** On the *Ditian Sui Chanwei*. The strength of the day stem is the first test: strong wants check, drain and exhaust, weak wants support. Quick to learn, and the most widely spread.

**Season.** On the *Qiongtong Baojian*. Neither strength nor pattern, but the weather of the birth month, and which stem has the qi of that month. A view of its own.

**The blind school.** No fixed text; passed by word of mouth among blind practitioners. Formulae and quick judgment, little theory, and a name for flat pronouncements. Its core methods are still not taught outside.

### 4.4 How to learn the subject

> I have studied, practised and taught this subject for more than twenty years. I have taken wrong turnings and watched a great many students give up halfway. Four things, bought with those twenty years.

**One: there is no shortcut.** Steady reading, a great deal of practice, and the habit of summing up. One chart a day for three months, and you will be a different student.

**Two: one school, deeply.** The field is full of voices, and every school that has lasted has something in it. What ruins a student is taking a little from each. Choose one, work at it for three years, and only then take in the rest. The best students I have had were, without exception, the ones who fixed on one school at the start and would not be moved.

**Three: understanding over memorising.** There is a great deal to remember and learning by rote yields little. Tao Yuanming: “I love reading and do not seek to understand it all; whenever something comes clear, I forget to eat.” Learn the name, learn what it means, and call on it in practice until it settles.

**Four: two things to avoid.** Repeating what one has heard without checking it, and raising one’s own school by running down another’s. Take what is good, and hold it with the rest.

Those four are what twenty years of teaching has left me with. The subject is not hard to learn; it is hard to keep at. Over the fourteen lessons that follow, take one step at a time.

## 5. Those good at the Changes do not divine

The phrase reads, on its face, that a person skilled in divination does not lightly cast. Its real sense is the opposite: the adept has no need to cast, because everything is a hexagram already.

A real master carries heaven and earth in him, and every movement and stillness is a hexagram. Someone knocks at dusk to borrow a thing, and the season tells you what it is. Spring returns and the grass comes up, and you know the ten thousand things are waking. A man comes in glowing and upright, and you know his hour is good. Not casting does not mean not reading; it means that the formal act is unnecessary.

### 5.1 Shao Yong and the borrowed axe

On a winter evening, at the you hour, someone knocked at Shao Yong’s door to borrow something. He asked his son to work out what. The son got the hexagram Gou, changing to Xun, and said: metal short, wood long, a tool; a hoe. Shao Yong said no, an axe. The door was opened and it was an axe.

The son asked why not a hoe. The answer is the good part of the story:

> “To set up the number one must understand the principle. By the hexagram, an axe will do and a hoe will do; by principle, who hoes at nightfall? It must be an axe, for splitting firewood. Setting up the number still requires understanding the principle, and that is the heart of divination.”

The point is plain. From the hexagram alone, axe and hoe both fit, since both are short metal on long wood. Add the circumstances — who hoes a field at dusk — and only the axe will do. Principle matters more than number.

### 5.2 A reading in the field

At the twentieth conference on the study of the Changes I gave a demonstration reading on stage. Afterwards Mr Yang of Chongqing came up with a group of fellow students and asked a difficult question: in a dispute then running, would he be drawn in?

I told him at once that he would be, that the effect would be small, and that the outcome was containable. The next day he reported that one of his associates had given himself up. I told him how to handle what followed, and it passed off.

What was the principle? It lay in a glance. Had he come alone, he could have stood clear. He came with a group, which already showed that many were involved and the matter was spreading — the circumstances. His own part in the dispute was marginal and shallow — the human reading. Put the two together, and the conclusion is there. No casting, no chart: the look of the party at the door told the shape of the thing.

The *Xici* says that the Changes have no thought and no action, that they are still and unmoving, and that by response they reach the affairs of the world. Reaching by response: not by calculation, but by a sharp sense of what a thing is, arriving straight at the conclusion. That is the phrase in modern practice, and the highest point of the art.

### 5.3 What the two cases teach

Shao Yong’s axe is a thousand years old; the reading above is recent. One ancient, one modern, and the same point: not divining means taking everything as a hexagram, and reading the sign together with the circumstances.

> A note by Chujia Kuang. The heart of the subject is one word, principle. The same chart, in another period, another region, another family, produces different events. The worst fault in judging is to apply a formula dead: the sign tells you what it looks like, the circumstances tell you what it is. Neither is enough alone.

## 6. A route through the course

### 6.1 Practice is the only test

Think, practise, sum up: that is the only road. Do not read without practising. Take your own chart first, then those of family and friends. After each reading write down what was right and what was wrong, so that there is a loop: read, practise, sum up, read again.

### 6.2 Three capacities to build

**One: the aim of a reading is to take the good and avoid the ill.** The result is a means, not an end. What is done with it is the point. The sequence runs: set up the chart, judge, obtain the result, apply it, arrive at the purpose.

**Two: accumulation, with no fast track.** One chart a day for three months, and your reading of people sharpens. Build a file of cases and record the feedback each time. Go back over them: what was right, what was wrong, and why.

**Three: keep the reading close to social reality.** A reading cut off from the world it is given in is worthless; see sections 7.5 and 7.6. Wealth was once stipend and land; it is now salary, investments, shares, digital currency. The meaning of the wealth star has moved, and holding to the old measure misreads the present.

### 6.3 Four stages

**Stage one, one week: yin and yang, the five phases, the stems and branches** (this lesson and lessons 2 to 4). A quick memory can do it in a week. The aim is to have the phases of the stems and branches and their generation and check at one’s fingertips; everything later rests on it.

**Stage two, three to four weeks: the ten gods and the twelve stages** (lessons 6 to 13). The ten gods are the grammar of a chart: they decide how stems and branches combine and how they are read. The work here is to understand the several faces of each god — the same direct officer is authority at work and pressure in health — and not to memorise lists.

**Stage three, about a month: strength and decline, shensha, the cycles** (lessons 14 to 17). The change from reading signs to judging fortune. Strength tells you how strong the day master is; the cycles tell you when it will act. Practise on your own chart and your family’s.

**Stage four, ongoing: reading and cases.** One chart a day for three months. The general view is that from no knowledge to judging independently takes two or three years of study and a great deal of practice. Seventeen lessons are the starting line; the practice outside them is the track.

> A note by Chujia Kuang. Finishing seventeen lessons is a beginning. The real work is outside the course: one chart a day for three months, and you will be changed. This art is not learned, it is practised. In the years when I was posting readings on the old forums, every chart added a little to the understanding. One phrase runs through this course from end to end: one body, many faces. Learn that, and you have learned how to look at a chart. It is like any practice: it will not be hurried.

## 7. Pitfalls in practice

> What follows is drawn from my own work. Every item is a hole I have fallen into.

### 7.1 Begin with what you are sure of

There is a pattern in reading: take first the thing you are most sure of. When the person confirms it, confidence rises and the mind loosens into connected thinking. It is like a speech: a good opening and the rest flows; a stumble at the start and the rest is fluster. Keep the room quiet as well; interruptions break the thread.

### 7.2 Where the reading is bad, weigh the words

Where you see something clearly bad, choose the wording. Nothing in this work is certain: it is reasoning, and reasoning is never wholly exact. Even when sure, put it gently.

A friend once read charts at a weekend market. A couple came; the woman gave her chart, and he said at once, “at such an age your father died.” She said he was alive and well. He answered, to himself but aloud, “strange, why is her father not dead?” and there was a scene.

The lesson: think before a verdict leaves the mouth, above all where health and kin are concerned. Better vague than flat.

### 7.3 Clear charts are read, muddy ones are not

What matters in a chart is clear against muddy, and feeling against no feeling. Clear is water you can see the bottom of: the pattern plain, the useful god evident. Muddy is water with something hidden in it: pattern mixed, useful god unclear, the signs blurred. The higher the chart, the easier it reads; the lower, the harder. If you look and look and it stays tangled, the chart is probably not of a high order.

### 7.4 Watch for daylight saving time

China kept daylight saving time from 1986 to 1991. Charts for births in those years need care; see the procedure in 1.2.

### 7.5 The period cannot be ignored

A chart is fixed; the period decides whether what is in it can be cashed. The same pattern in two periods produces utterly different events.

Take the wealth star. A chart strong in wealth whose wealth decade fell in the ten years of the Cultural Revolution, when private trade had all but gone, could not use it: the most that could be said was enough to eat. The same pattern after the reforms may be a business empire. That is policy holding a chart down, or letting it go.

Marriage customs likewise. In the 1960s and 70s marrying at twenty-six was late; in cities now, unmarried at thirty is ordinary. A chart once read as hard on the husband may now show only a late marriage or a difficult one. The ten-god reading of marriage has not changed; society’s definition of marriage has.

Work and mobility move with the period too. The direct officer once pointed to the examinations and office; now it covers the civil service, managers in firms, professional qualifications. The same officer pattern may be an official inside the system, a manager in business, or a certified professional — the god unchanged, the social role otherwise.

Period and region, one vertical and one horizontal, are the external conditions under which a chart is cashed. To judge without weighing the times is to mark the boat to find the sword.

### 7.6 The region cannot be ignored

Regions differ in wealth, in politics, in custom and in manner. A reading cut off from the region is as good as none.

Wealth matters most. Born in Shenzhen and born in Jieyang, the economic base is not comparable. The same wealth-strong chart may be a fortune in a first-tier city and no more than comfort in a mountain district. Judge wealth without asking where, and you will be far out.

Marriage custom varies. In parts of Guizhou and Guangxi people marry early, often at sixteen or seventeen, straight out of middle school. Judge by the urban standard of late marriage and you will be wrong.

Social climate varies too. The three north-eastern provinces lead the country in divorce and trail in births; Guangdong is steadier on both. The same difficult marriage chart weighs differently in the two.

Culture shapes direction. Shandong and Henan and thereabouts value propriety and the examinations; Shanghai values appearance and taste; Guangdong is practical and low-key. The same direct officer pattern may point to the system in Shandong and to trade in Guangdong — one god, different direction, because the ground differs.

In practice, weigh the economy, the politics and the customs of the place. The chart is the body, the region the use; body and use together give the reading.

## Summary of the lesson

1. The eight characters: the year, month, day and hour of birth written in stems and branches, and the starting point of the analysis.
2. Fate and luck: fate is given and does not change; luck is the course afterwards and moves; together they give the shape of a life.
3. From the Zhouyi to the chart: eight trigrams, then the five phases, then the stems and branches, then three pillars, then four.
4. Schools and texts: pattern, illness and medicine, strength and decline, season, and the blind school, each with something to it; depth in one is the right road.
5. Those good at the Changes do not divine: everything is a hexagram, and principle matters more than number.
6. A route through: practice is the only test, four stages from first principles to independent reading, and the habit of summing up.
7. Pitfalls: careful wording, clear against muddy, and the period and the region always weighed.

## Practice

### Exercise 1: the four pillars

Which four pillars make up a chart, and what does each stand for?

1. Year (ancestors), month (parents), day (self), hour (children).
2. Year (self), month (ancestors), day (children), hour (parents).
3. Year (parents), month (self), day (children), hour (ancestors).
4. Year (children), month (parents), day (self), hour (ancestors).

???+ "Answer"
    A. The year pillar is the root and the ancestors; the month is the frame and the parents; the day is the centre and the self; the hour is the branch and the children.

### Exercise 2: fate and luck

Which statement about fate and luck is right?

1. A person of poor fate can never succeed.
2. Luck matters more than fate, and fate can be disregarded.
3. Fate is the frame fixed before birth, luck the course that moves afterwards, and the two together decide the shape of a life.
4. Both are fixed and neither can be changed.

???+ "Answer"
    C. Fate is what is given, and sets the basic capacity and the ceiling; luck is the course afterwards and sets how favourable each stage is. Good fate with good luck runs smooth; good fate with bad luck is the right person at the wrong time; ordinary fate with good luck still gets somewhere.

### Exercise 3: the phrase

What does “those good at the Changes do not divine” actually mean?

1. A person skilled in the Changes has no need to cast.
2. Heaven and earth are within him and anything can be read, so the formal act is unnecessary.
3. Divination is useless, so it is not done.
4. Only beginners need to cast.

???+ "Answer"
    B. It does not mean that nothing is read. Everything can serve as the instrument, and to others it looks like no divination at all. Principle matters more than number, as Shao Yong’s borrowed axe shows.

### Exercise 4: the schools

Which of these books belongs to the school of strength and decline?

1. *Yuanhai Ziping*
2. *Ditian Sui Chanwei*
3. *Qiongtong Baojian*
4. *Shenfeng Tongkao*

???+ "Answer"
    B. The *Yuanhai Ziping* belongs to the pattern school, Xu Dasheng in the Song; the *Ditian Sui Chanwei* to strength and decline, Ren Tieqiao in the Qing; the *Qiongtong Baojian* to the season school, late Qing; the *Shenfeng Tongkao* to illness and medicine, Zhang Shenfeng in the Ming.

### Exercise 5: application

Why is a reading cut off from social reality worthless? Give an example.

???+ "Answer"
    The chart method was summed up from the society its writers lived in, and carried to another society without adjustment it misreads. Wealth once meant stipend, land and goods in hand; it now means salary, investments, shares, digital currency and business income. Read a present-day fortune by the old sense of the wealth star and the answer will be wrong. Learn the old theory, and judge by the present world.
